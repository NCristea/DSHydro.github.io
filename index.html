DSHydro

Data Science and Hydrology

Our research combines field and remotely sensed observations with physics-based and machine-learning models to understand large- and fine-scale hydrologic phenomena in a changing climate. We are particularly interested in transferring these methods across different scales and geographic settings. Given the growing importance of efficiently storing, analyzing, and visualizing large datasets, we are integrating modern data science techniques into our research.

Research

Our research themes and approaches

Hydrology & Ecohydrology

Detecting Snowmelt Phases with SAR and Hydrological Modeling

Monitoring the physical state of the snowpack is essential for predicting spring runoff and managing water supplies. We leverage Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)—which can penetrate cloud cover—in combination with robust hydrological models to track liquid water content in the snowpack. This integrated approach allows us to reliably detect critical snowmelt phases, such as the onset of runoff, improving early warning systems for floods and water availability forecasts.

Detecting Tree Mortality in Forests

Tree mortality driven by climate stress, drought, and insect outbreaks significantly impacts forest health, watershed dynamics, and carbon cycling. We leverage foundation models combined with high-resolution satellite imagery to detect, map, and monitor forest tree mortality across broad spatial scales. By harnessing computer vision foundation models and fine-resolution remote sensing observations, our work enables scalable, precise identification of canopy decline and dead trees, advancing forest monitoring, wildfire risk assessment, and ecohydrological research.

Extreme Events

We leverage gridded datasets, physics-based hydrologic models, and machine learning techniques to analyze and quantify extreme environmental events across varying spatial and temporal scales. We integrate multi-source observational data with predictive algorithms to advance the characterization, risk assessment, and monitoring of severe phenomena including floods, droughts, and tornadoes.

Identifying Snow-Covered Areas at High Spatial Resolution

Accurate mapping of snow-covered areas is critical for understanding water resources and ecological dynamics in mountainous regions. Our research develops and applies advanced remote sensing techniques to capture snow distribution at meter scale spatial resolutions. By refining these observational methods, we can bridge the gap between sparse ground measurements and broad climate models, capturing micro-topographic effects on snow accumulation and ablation.

Data-Driven Modeling

Machine Learning for Streamflow and Snowpack Representation

Traditional hydrological models can be computationally expensive and rely heavily on precise physical parameterization. To overcome these limitations, we utilize advanced machine learning algorithms designed to capture complex, non-linear relationships in environmental datasets. These models allow for highly accurate, fast predictions of streamflow dynamics and snowpack evolution across varying spatial and temporal scales.

Physics-Informed Machine Learning Modeling

While purely data-driven approaches are powerful, they can sometimes yield predictions that violate fundamental physical laws. Our research addresses this by embedding physical principles—such as mass and energy conservation—directly into neural network architectures. These physics-informed machine learning (PIML) models ensure that our predictions are not only highly accurate but also physically realistic, making them more robust when forecasting unprecedented climate extremes or unobserved conditions.

Interdisciplinary Research

Merging Hydrology, Seismology, and Modern AI

Our work crosses traditional disciplinary boundaries to unlock new environmental monitoring capabilities. By integrating hydrological processes with environmental seismology, we use modern artificial intelligence and advanced cyberinfrastructure to analyze continuous seismic data. This allows us to extract meaningful signals from ambient seismic “noise,” turning ground vibrations into valuable proxy data for monitoring river turbulence, bedload transport, and extreme hydrological events at high temporal resolutions.

Data Science

Integrating machine learning, cloud computing, and AI education in geosciences

Data Science and AI Seminars and Training at the UW eScience Institute

Nicoleta is a data science fellow at the eScience Institute, where she hosts the UW Data Science & AI Tricampus seminar (Seattle, Bothell and Tacoma), an annual lecture series at the University of Washington that hosts scholars working across applied areas of data science and AI, such as the arts, engineering, humanities and sciences along with methodological areas in data science, such as computer science, applied math and statistics. Students may opt to sign up for a one credit seminar (CR/NCR) listed as ENGR 591. At the eScience Institute, Nicoleta is also contributing to training programs such as the Data Science and AI Accelerator program and hackweek events.

Mentoring Data Science Capstone Projects

We actively participate in team mentoring for student projects in the UW Master of Science in Data Science Capstone Program. The capstone mentorship provides guidance to graduate student teams working on complex data-driven challenges, with projects including applications of machine learning, interactive data visualization, and reproducible research in the geosciences.

ML and Data Science Curriculum in Geosciences

Machine learning applications in water resources and other geoscience fields are increasingly used to advance data-driven discovery. To facilitate faster adoption of these techniques GeoSMART, an NSF-funded educational and research initiative led by PI Cristea at the University of Washington, was dedicated to integrating machine learning (ML) and cloud computing into the geosciences. The program supported courses at UW, such as Machine Learning in the Geosciences (ESS 469/569, which utilize open-source Jupyter Notebooks, cloud- and Python-based workflows), as well as hands-on training events (hackweeks), end-to-end machine learning use cases, and review articles. Although the project has ended, we remain committed to maintaining and updating the GeoSMART website.

Applied Research

Partnering with stakeholders to inform water and climate decisions

Current and Future Hydrology of Seattle City Light Managed Basins

Ongoing changes in snowpack, linked to long-term climate projections, affect the region's water resources and mountain ecosystems, posing new challenges for water resource managers. Through this collaboration, we evaluate future snowmelt trends in the South Fork Tolt River basin and develop meteorological metrics from gridded datasets for the Skagit River basin and other Washington locations. We analyze historical observations, climate projections, hydrologic model outputs, and key meteorological variables to support Seattle City Light's climate adaptation, water resource, and energy planning.

CIVIC NSF Stage 1

We develop a research-centered pilot project in partnership with the UW Climate Impacts Group to evaluate how forest management practices may affect summer low flows in Pacific Northwest watersheds, with a focus on salmon habitat, Tribal treaty rights, and climate resilience. The project will bring together scientists, Tribal representatives, resource managers, NGOs, and other partners to design applied field and modeling research on how forest age, thinning, canopy gaps, and snow storage influence streamflow.

Supporting Regional Stakeholders with Hydrologic Modeling, Climate Analysis and Projections of Future Hydrology

We collaborate with the UW Climate Impacts Group to leverage the Distributed Hydrology Soil Vegetation Model (DHSVM), a high-resolution, physics-based model that translates broad Global Climate Models into precise, localized watershed projections. By incorporating fine-scale data like topography, soil depth, and forest canopy, DHSVM simulates how future climate scenarios will impact processes like snowmelt, soil moisture, and streamflow. The Climate Impacts Group (CIG) uses these simulations to assess specific regional risks, including shifting flood vulnerabilities, water supply shortages, and ecosystem changes.

Nicoleta is a research assistant professor in the Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering and a senior data science fellow at the eScience Institute at the University of Washington. Her work integrates geographic information systems, satellite imagery, spatial statistics, spatiotemporal visualization, and machine learning.
